Why does Lei Jun keep getting criticized? The answer goes back to 2011, the year Xiaomi and its consumers signed an invisible contract: be your friend, price honestly. Ever since, the public's tolerance for his mistakes has sat below that granted to any other entrepreneur. The same fine print, the same modest price hike — at another brand it is normal business; at Xiaomi it is betrayal, a rip-off. That is not a double standard. It is contract terms: promise what others never promised, and submit to scrutiny others never face.

## How the Contract Becomes a Cage

The contract's first premium was the publicity, and its first penalty clause is the same publicity. Early on, Lei Jun deliberately benchmarked himself against Jobs, Musk, and Jensen Huang to build an industry persona; the public formed a fixed gaze as a result. Today, whatever he wears gets force-read as copying. The labels that once generated buzz now hold his wardrobe hostage. The same applies to his folksy image. In the early years, posting on forums and visiting stores was a bottom-up personal habit; later it became a hard promotional KPI — multi-camera shoots, professional sets, assembly-line staging — and one bystander's blunt remark is enough to puncture the performance. Once sincerity moves from habit to budget line, the audience can smell it.

The contract's coldest clause: phones can slip by, cars cannot. Vague marketing and fine-print tricks in the phone industry were tolerated because the cost was money. Cars involve personal safety; the same operations escalate instantly into a safety credibility crisis, with accountability and backlash multiplying. The three days of silence after the SU7 fire are the case sample: delaying a public statement was a compliance requirement — wait for the official investigation, avoid legal exposure — unimpeachable on legal grounds. But under the be-your-friend expectation, silence was read directly as cold betrayal, and public sentiment turned from Lei-god to Lei-hypocrite. One behavior, two readings; the only difference is whether the contract exists.

## The Man Who Tore Up the Contract

The most damaging scene played out in the flavor-cooler-lid trial. For every controversy before it, Lei Jun had personally taken the blame — that was the contract being honored. But in court, Xiaomi's legal team severed corporate messaging from personal statements, asserting that his social-media remarks carried no binding force and that he did not understand the product's structure. Perhaps legally precise; commercially it was the company announcing, in its own voice, that a promise from Lei Jun is not a promise from Xiaomi. The most valuable clause of the 2011 contract, torn up by the company's own lawyers.

Why can't he exit? The comparison group stands in plain sight: Jack Ma exited smoothly; Tim Cook never has to face the public; Musk's controversies never touch his base. Lei Jun is welded to two consumer-facing IPs — Xiaomi hardware and Xiaomi Auto — where his personal image directly determines sales. There is no exit. A persona, signed to the end, makes the signatory and the company identical; tearing it up would mean tearing the company. The contract also embeds an industry clause: extreme value pricing keeps compressing margins across the whole handset industry, limiting R&D money and the long climb toward Apple's high-end wall; ecosystem companies like Roborock and Huami, once grown, raced to de-Xiaomi themselves, because channel, pricing, and brand power all sit in Xiaomi's hands, replaceable at will. The contract runs both ways: he surrendered profit to consumers, and he took independence from partners. A persona is never a free asset. It is a contract that never finishes being paid.
