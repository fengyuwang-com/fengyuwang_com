History offers a coincidence: two men named Wang An, nine hundred years apart, dead of the same disease. One built a company from a Boston garage to a valuation that overtook IBM, then went bankrupt within six years. The other turned an empire in a new direction, then watched his reforms repealed one day at a time after his death. Hang their stories side by side, and the diagnosis sheet is the same.

## Wang An, 1979

How high was Wang Laboratories at its peak? Three billion US dollars in monthly revenue, a market cap that briefly outweighed IBM, its founder the richest Chinese person alive. The company started on magnetic-core memory — Wang sold the invention outright to IBM for half a million dollars and used that money to open shop; a single product line of office word processors beat IBM at its own game. In 1979, his vice president proposed building personal computers. Wang's answer: "Personal computers? An absurdity never heard of!" He made specialized office machines; whatever households and hobbyists wanted wasn't worth stooping for. By the time he followed in 1982, he insisted on a proprietary machine incompatible with IBM's. By the time he conceded compatibility, the IBM PC was the standard — three years late.

The more fatal step came after. In 1986, Wang, sixty-six and diagnosed with cancer, pushed his eldest son into the president's chair against the united opposition of his board and executives. His reasoning: "Because I am the founder, I want to keep complete control of the company, and give my children the chance to prove they can run it." Losses of over 400 million by 1988; bankruptcy in 1992. Peak to death: six years.

## Wang Anshi, 1074

The other Wang's window was called reform. His slogans were harder than anyone had dared in two centuries: heaven's omens need not be feared, ancestral precedents need not be copied, men's words need not be heeded. The twenty-something emperor followed him devotedly, and the new laws landed one after another. Then the emperor wavered — everyone outside says the reforms are bad, what do we do? Wang Anshi pushed back, but from that day the string between ruler and minister had loosened. Dismissed as chief councillor, recalled, then discovering that a protégé he had raised was maneuvering behind his back to bring him down — and the emperor, when he tried to purge the man, refused. In 1076 his son died young; he was dismissed again and retired to Mount Zhong. In 1086 the opposition took power wholesale and repealed the new laws one by one — he died that year, watching the things he had brought into the world collected and carried off.

## The Verdict

Whether Wang An or Wang Anshi, it comes down to one sentence: when it was time to change, he refused to change — and so he ended. The window Wang An refused was called the personal computer; the thing Wang Anshi defended to the last was a plan of his own that admitted no revision. One failed at succession, one failed at succession of doctrine — and what fails to be handed over is never really the power. It's the capacity to keep changing.

## The Opposition

Is this a master key? No. Some wanted to change and died anyway. Nokia wanted to — it leapt from Symbian to Windows Phone, both steps taken. Kodak's case is worse: the digital camera was Kodak's own invention. Those who wanted to change and still died, died of the wrong posture, the wrong timing, a lane off the main road. So the sentence is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one: refusing to change means death; changing guarantees nothing.

## The Ledger

So how do you tell whose change survives? Look at the books. A young company can afford to lose, so it never runs this calculation — it just commits. An old company can no longer afford to lose, so it runs the preservation arithmetic on everything — and the new thing stays forever in next fiscal year. Wang An was calculating word-processor margins in that 1986 boardroom; Wang Anshi was defending the proposition that his system was right. Neither was guarding the wrong thing. But the posture of guarding barred the door to anything new.

In a 1986 conference room, Wang An ran the numbers, and every line said win. When the end came, the books still said win.
