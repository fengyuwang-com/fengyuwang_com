Can we say it plainly? Microsoft's victory in personal computing was not a victory of product ability, nor of programming brilliance. It won purely by being early.

Being early was the foundation. IBM came to buy an operating system; Microsoft bought a rough clone, renamed it MS-DOS, and kept the right to license it to clone makers. That one move seized the standard — everything after was rent collection.

In fairness, though: being early alone doesn't win thirty years. Microsoft's real craft was welding a merely-adequate product onto the standard position: OEM preinstalls, backward compatibility, antitrust-grade bundling. It won on distribution and lock-in, not on product aesthetics.

The ARM deal is the most revealing: Microsoft signed ARM exclusively to Qualcomm — the same script as Intel's exclusive x86. ARM in its day was buildable by anyone; Texas Instruments built it, Apple built it, and that openness is exactly why it became a standard. An exclusive contract turns an open standard back into a private road.

Being first is a lottery ticket; lock-in is a craft. So judge a company not by whether it arrived early, but by what it did after arriving: pave the road for everyone, or weld the tollbooth to its own gate.
