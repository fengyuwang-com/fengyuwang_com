Tribute to Laozi

Eight characters from 2,500 years ago that expose the deepest truth in finance

Robbing the Poor to Feed the Rich

The way of man — laid bare by Laozi 2,500 years ago

Chapter 77 of the Dao De Jing: "The Way of Heaven takes from the surplus and gives to the deficient. The way of man is not so — he robs the不足 (bùzú) to feed the有余 (yǒuyú)." In eight characters: 损不足以奉有余.

In plain language: nature trims the excess and supplements the deficient; but human society runs in the opposite direction — stripping those who already have too little to enrich those who already have too much.

This is the direct ancestor of a folk discovery called the "Dog Money Theorem." When one person's money decreases tragically, another person's increases comically — Laozi named this structure in the 6th century BC.

"Robbing the poor to feed the rich" describes the outcome: wealth flowing from the poor to the rich. The Dog Money Theorem describes the mechanism: some parties pocket the gains while bearing none of the losses. Laozi says where the money goes; the theorem says why. Two sides of the same coin.

Money Doesn't Disappear — It Moves

When wealth is lost, who takes the gains without sharing the risk?

The formal definition of the Dog Money Theorem: when wealth is lost, there exist parties who have already extracted real gains from the transaction chain, yet bear zero cost for this loss. These parties are the "dogs."

In economic language: profits are privatized, losses are externalized. These ten words are the layman's translation of Coase's core insight — the one that won him the 1991 Nobel Prize in Economics.

The Xu family cashed out 54 billion HKD in dividends. The 2.44-trillion-yuan hole was absorbed by homebuyers, suppliers, and taxpayers. Local governments collected 963.4 billion in land-transfer fees — not a cent returned. Financial institutions reaped hundreds of billions in interest and are now writing off bad debt, while losses are split between shareholders and depositors. Evergrande Wealth executives redeemed all their wealth products before the collapse — these dogs snatched the meat right before the crash.

This is exactly what Laozi meant: those who pocket the gains never pay for the losses; those who bear the losses never received the gains.

A Typhoon Blows Away the Pomelos. Someone Profits.

A natural disaster is not the end — it's where redistribution begins

In August 2026, Typhoon "White Dolphin" hit Zhejiang's Taizhou. Yuhuan's pomelo heartland was flooded. Of the city's 29,000 mu of pomelo cultivation, 20,000 mu went underwater. Yanggen Village — "China's pomelo village number one" — saw nearly 40% of its economic output wiped out, around 20 million yuan. Fruit that fell to the ground was a total loss. The floodwater rotted most of what it touched.

The crop perished. But the "money" didn't. The farmers' "expected money" didn't vanish — through price increases, it was transferred to four destinations:

  • Undamaged farmers elsewhere: pomelos in Fujian's Xianyou and Taiwan's Hualien automatically rose in price as supply contracted
  • Wholesalers who locked in supply pre-typhoon: bought at pre-disaster prices, sold at post-disaster market rates, pocketed the spread
  • Agricultural input suppliers: demand for post-disaster pesticides and treatments spiked
  • Consumers: paid 30%–50% more per jin of pomelo than last year

In a market economy, scarcity is itself a dog. When supply shrinks due to disaster, scarcity automatically redirects money from the damaged to the survivors. No malice required — the system completes the "dog grab" on its own. Laozi said "the Way of Heaven takes from the surplus to supplement the deficient" — but in reality, markets usually follow the way of man.

Laozi's Economic Pedigree

Not late — 2,600 years early

"I do nothing, and the people transform themselves"

Hayek, 1974 Nobel laureate in Economics, called Laozi his "intellectual father." The core of spontaneous order — that markets self-regulate, and heavy-handed intervention disrupts the balance — Laozi said it in eight characters, 2,600 years ago.

"Take from the surplus, supplement the deficient"

The law of large numbers in modern insurance — spreading individual catastrophic risk across many small contributors — is nothing more than institutionalized "surplus supplementing deficiency." Laozi is the philosophical prototype of insurance.

"He who knows sufficiency is rich"

Behavioral finance discovered that investors, driven by "loss aversion," sell winning stocks and hold losing ones. Laozi said it long ago: knowing when to stop matters more than knowing when to start.

Laozi Doesn't Need a Nobel Prize

He needs no award to prove he was right

The Nobel goes to those who prove a proposition with rigorous methods. Laozi is the one who intuits a proposition directly. In five thousand characters, he hit several of the deepest questions in human knowledge — 2,600 years before anyone began systematically asking them.

Hayek's spontaneous order theory? Laozi said it in eight characters: "I do nothing, and the people transform themselves." The law of large numbers in insurance? Six characters: "Take from the surplus, supplement the deficient." Bounded rationality in behavioral economics? Four: "He who knows sufficiency is rich."

Laozi was not late. He was 2,600 years early. So early that the Nobel Committee didn't exist, economics wasn't yet a discipline, and the concept of "the market" would not be reinvented by Adam Smith for another two millennia.

Laozi's five thousand characters pierced through 2,500 years. This is not prophecy. It is the crushing force of pure insight.
