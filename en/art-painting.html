Empty Space & Essence

The essence of Chinese landscape painting is negative space.

Whether it's "A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains", "Dwelling in the Fuchun Mountains", or "Along the River During the Qingming Festival"—they don't pile on detail; they leave room for the imagination. A single painting can depict a thousand li of river and mountain, but the real craft isn't in what was painted. It's in what was left unpainted.

Picasso's path went the opposite way. Early on he pursued accuracy almost to the level of photography—a faithful rendering of reality, tight control of detail. But the later he went, the less he drew. A few lines and an idea appears. Not because he couldn't draw, but because he was asking: how much is enough?

These two roads—Chinese landscape painting building on white space from the start, and Picasso spending a lifetime arriving at minimalism—converge at the same point: good abstraction isn't about removing detail. It's about keeping only the details that matter.

Spirit Over Resemblance

Standing before Wu Weishan's Lu Xun statue, the first thing you see is not a copy. You see the brush-like moustache, the arrogance that defies everything, the will to topple the old society. He didn't reproduce Lu Xun's likeness, but he kept Lu Xun—kept the detail that mattered.

David's sculpture isn't a faithful rendering of a male body either. What you see is the spirit of a challenger, the posture of facing a giant anyway. A marble statue of a naked man—what you feel isn't flesh; it's resolve.

The pharaoh statues of ancient Egypt went even further. They don't chase "likeness"; they chase "authority". The pharaoh's eyes always look straight ahead, the body eternally seated, the posture never changing—it says: what you face is not a man, but an order. The ancient Egyptians shaped into their sculpture a reverence for the divine and a faith in the afterlife.

So the essence of art isn't resemblance. It's whether the spirit is there.
