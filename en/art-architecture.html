Architecture & Garden

Good architecture never sacrifices function for form, and never sacrifices spirit for utility.

The Spirit of Modern Architecture

The Shanghai Tower's spiral rise gives a feeling of endless ascent—this is modern architecture speaking. It uses the simplest geometry to express an attitude: upward, without pause.

Shenzhen's "Spring Bamboo" and "Spring Cocoon" work the same way. One bursts out of the ground like a bamboo shoot; the other nests a life like a silkworm cocoon. They aren't buildings—they're symbols. The geometric facets of Hong Kong's Bank of China Tower and I.M. Pei's Suzhou Museum, welding Chinese garden aesthetics to modernist geometry, prove one thing: the Chinese spirit needs no retro facade; modern language can express the Eastern mood just as well.

This is where modern architecture is heavier than many assume. It lost classical ornament, but it didn't lose its spirit.

Form and Function Were Never Separate

Classical architecture is ornate—Greek and Roman orders, European carvings, the bracket-sets and painted beams of Chinese palaces. These aren't mere decoration; they carry ritual, power, and belief.

But the Chinese curved roof is a particularly good example. It bends, arcs, is capped with tiles and ridge-beasts—it looks purely decorative, yet it does three things at once: drains water, keeps birds from nesting, and displays the owner's social status. It is both tool and symbol, born one and indivisible.

Modern architecture took them apart, and many then threw away the symbol side—that's the Khrushchyovka you see: pure pragmatism, spirit lost.

The curved roof teaches me: function and form never had to fight. You don't sacrifice one for the other.

Harmony with Nature

I once sat in the Waning Villa at Wu Zhen, the home of Mu Xin. The pavilion, the trees, the river—nothing was "designed" on purpose, yet you felt you were part of nature, not its master.

The subtlety of Suzhou gardens lies here. They don't ask you to look at scenery—they place you inside it. Every window frames a painting, every path curves, every step leads you into a relationship with the natural world.

The Chinese idea of "harmony between heaven and man" isn't a rhetorical device; it's a worldview: humans don't come to conquer nature, they come to coexist with it. This idea is carved into the bones of Chinese architecture—but in modern architecture, except for a few rare exceptions, it has nearly vanished.

Good architecture reminds you: you are part of this world, not its center.
