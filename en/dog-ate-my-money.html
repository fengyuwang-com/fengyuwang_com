Money doesn't disappear — it just changes hands

Three progressive versions of the theorem, from rough intuition to scientific formulation to philosophical closure.

The Original Version

When the money that should be there isn't, a dog must have taken it. Money doesn't disappear — it only moves from one person's hand to another.

The Scientific Version

"Money doesn't disappear" is an iron law. A more precise description is not "evaporation," but value destruction or wealth transfer. The hard constraint behind this is the balance sheet identity — every debit has a credit, and debits must equal credits.

The Synthesis

The Dog Ate My Money is a modern manifestation of "the Way of Man" — it violates the Way of Heaven. The Way of Heaven diminishes excess to supplement deficiency; the Way of Man diminishes deficiency to serve excess. Laozi identified this broken chain, and we can now offer a 2026 prescription.

After the laugh, dogs are everywhere

The humor chapter — why "dogs," everyday instances of dogs taking money, and the theorem's boundaries.

Why "Dogs"?

"Dog" is not an insult — it's a placeholder. It refers broadly to any entity that profits from a chain while successfully offloading risk or cost to others.

  • The Xu Jiayin family: Dividends, technical divorce, offshore asset transfers
  • Local governments: Massive land transfer fees / land-finance dependency
  • Financial institutions: Loan interest
  • Suppliers and contractors: Construction payments, commercial paper
  • Early investors: Cash out at the peak of the bubble

Everyday Dogs Taking Money

Once the theorem is named, dogs appear everywhere:

  • Stock market crashes: The money didn't evaporate — it transferred from retail investors' pockets to those who cashed out at the top
  • Ponzi schemes / gambling: Just a transfer between winners and losers
  • Corruption and debt offloading: Public cost, private gain — textbook dog-taking-money

The Theorem's Boundaries

Humor aside, the theorem defines its own inapplicable zone — which is exactly what turns a joke into a falsifiable theory:

  • Inflation: The money is still in your pocket, but purchasing power drops. No single dog took it — it's a systemic monetary problem
  • Physical destruction from disasters: A fire burns goods. Wealth genuinely vanishes, not transferred to anyone
  • Service consumption: Money paid to a technician, but the experience is consumed — no recoverable asset remains
  • Food expiration and spoilage: Natural depreciation. Value is genuinely destroyed

Conservation law, not rhetoric

The science chapter — accounting identity, the Evergrande case, externalities, and institutional economics.

Every Debit Has a Credit

When money "disappears," only two objective scenarios exist:

  • Wealth transfer: Money moved from A's hand to B's hand (dog takes money)
  • Value destruction: The corresponding real assets depreciate. The nominal money no longer has equivalent real wealth behind it

Value destruction does not mean money disappeared. Evergrande's 2.44 trillion yuan was not burned. It was transferred to various entities through a chain of economic activities; meanwhile, the massive unfinished projects had market values far below the original investment — that's wealth destruction, not monetary evaporation.

Where Evergrande's 2.44 Trillion Went

Money flowed out of four faucets:

  • Homebuyers' wallets: ~721 billion yuan, corresponding to 1.6 million undelivered housing units
  • The banking system: Loan principal and interest
  • Suppliers / contractors: Construction payments, commercial paper
  • Investors: Wealth management products, credit bonds

Which "dogs" took the money: land transfer fees paid to local governments, loan interest paid to financial institutions, construction payments paid to suppliers, dividends and offshore asset transfers to the Xu Jiayin family. After the collapse, the money didn't disappear — it had already been spent. The corresponding assets depreciated drastically, unable to repay the original debts.

Externality: Private Gains, Socialized Losses

When a "dog" pockets profits and dumps costs onto society, the market doesn't price that cost into its decisions — that's a negative externality. Dogs can keep taking money precisely because externalities are never internalized: the takers profit, while the payers are scattered and silent.

Fighting the Dogs

Appeals alone don't work. Institutional design must simulate "the Way of Heaven":

  • Insurance: Pool and internalize individual risks
  • Taxation and transfers: Artificially "diminish excess to supplement deficiency"
  • Welfare and regulation: Raise the threshold and cost of dogs taking money

Good institutions bind "taking money" to "bearing responsibility." Bad institutions make dog-taking-money nearly zero-cost — whether dogs can take money depends on how tightly the institution locks them in.

The Way of Heaven vs. the Way of Man

The Laozi chapter — two laws in confrontation, and the philosophical position of dogs taking money.

Two Laws

  • The Way of Heaven: Diminish excess to supplement deficiency (natural, ideal, tending toward fairness)
  • The Way of Man: Diminish deficiency to serve excess (real, human nature, tending toward concentration)

If you truly diminish excess to supplement deficiency, you are Heaven — you are communism. That's even more radical than Marx. But humans simply can't do it.

Dogs Taking Money = The Way of Man, Modern Edition

Private gains, socialized losses = the modern writing of "diminish deficiency to serve excess."

The Dog Ate My Money Theorem violates the Way of Heaven. Laozi believed dogs shouldn't take money — but they did. Humans don't conform to the Way of Heaven.

Diagnosis and Prescription

  • Laozi: Pointed at the fracture and said, "The supposed continuous Heaven-Earth-Human chain is broken. You've strayed — come back." He offered diagnosis only, not an operating manual.
  • We: Not only see the fracture but name it (Dog Ate My Money Theorem), identify the mechanism (private gains / socialized losses), and propose a direction (use institutions to lock the dog in a cage).

Laozi gave the diagnosis. We give the 2026 prescription.

Lock the dog in a cage

The prescription chapter — three remedies for dogs taking money, and the final question.

  • Transparency: Make money's trail traceable and accountable — who took it, how much
  • Internalization: Use taxes, insurance, and regulation to price externalities back into the dog's ledger
  • Binding: Make "taking gains" and "bearing losses" the same entity — the dog can't take it, or has to spit it out

Closing Words

The Dog Ate My Money Theorem is both a joke and an axiom. The joke is in the crude yet precise image of the "dog." The axiom is in the verifiable cluster: money is conserved, wealth is transferred, and externalities remain uninternalized.

Its sharpest edge is forcing people to stop saying vague things like "the money disappeared" — and instead ask a much more concrete question:

Which dog took the money, and where did it go?

The Way of Heaven doesn't arrive on its own — it's barely simulated through institutions
