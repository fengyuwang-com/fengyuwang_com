The Compliment Was Scheduled

A lecturer tells the story of buying glasses twenty-one years ago. Ruth, the optician, first had him try on frames that were anything but the style he asked for. Then she lined up three options. Option 1, she announced, was somewhat boring, ordinary. Option 2 was a little nicer. Option 3 was the game changer. And then she added: most likely, he was going to want Option 1. She was absolutely right — he had already taken out his wallet to pay. She stopped him there. Today, she said, she was going to sell him Option 3, because it was the right option. The customer walks to the register on his own, and she stops him with her own hands — that order of operations is worth remembering; it comes back later.

On his commute home that day, strangers complimented his new glasses. He took the first four at face value. It took five compliments before it dawned on him: Ruth had literally stationed somebody outside the store to praise customers as they walked out. That was when he called her a genius. The person stationed at the door was probably working an hourly shift. That hourly shift is what carried twenty-one years of driving.

The Best Experience Hides Its Design

Why did it take five compliments? Because the first four looked exactly like the real thing. The mark of good design is that no trace of designing shows. Someone will say the compliments waiting outside were hired theater — a script, not service, the customer being played. But the play was genuinely performed and the glasses were genuinely good. The decisive detail sits elsewhere: Ruth sold Option 3, not the most expensive pair in the shop. The script was written to walk the customer toward the right frame. Design and sincerity were never opponents; design is the transmission that sincerity drives through. Maya Angelou said it best: “They may forget what you said, but they will never forget how you made them feel.” The first half of that line comforts. The second half warns — the feeling can be arranged.

A Good Product Is Not Enough

Others will say the glasses were fine and the theater was superfluous — a good product speaks for itself. Good products speak everywhere. He did the arithmetic himself: twenty-one years of traveling three hours each way, taking a personal vacation day on a weekday, while a hundred optical centers sat closer and online options sat cheaper and more convenient, selling lenses every bit as competent. Same product — so why does the road run in one direction only? The product sets the passing grade, and the passing grade belongs to the whole industry; everyone has it. It cannot set a three-hour drive — that drive belongs to one shop alone. Competent products are everywhere. Being remembered is rare.

An Outlier, or Homework Done Daily

Then comes the survivorship objection: Ruth is an outlier, and preaching from outliers is taking lottery winners for financial advice. The objection looks solid, but in the same passage the lecturer lists her peers: Mitchell’s, opened in 1958; Ways, in 1964; Zingerman’s, in 1982 — all still alive after decades. One shop is luck. Several clustered together stops looking like luck. What they share is not an industry. It is treating experience as daily homework, not decoration. Forrester’s 2016 research points the same direction: experience leaders significantly outperform laggards on revenue.

Copy the Moves, Not the Judgment

So copy it. Hire the greeters, stage the three frames, recite the lines. None of it transfers — and it often transfers backwards. Ruth’s judgment hides inside an order of operations: she knew the customer would most likely reject Option 1, yet she let him walk to the register on his own first — then stopped him with her own hands. Let him choose first, then intervene; reverse the order and the play falls apart. The script is written live, for the person standing in front of her. Templates are dead; judgment is alive. Learning someone’s moves by heart never reproduces their timing.

Twenty-one years, three hours each way, a vacation day spent — for one pair of glasses, along a road with a hundred closer shops. Loyalty is not argued into existence. It is a feeling that was arranged, then fed, one day at a time.
