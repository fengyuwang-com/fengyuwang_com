Hunt Fast, Farm Slow

2026-09-11 · Investing

On the hottest trading days, what is the busiest person in the market chasing? News, limit-ups, the next big theme. Busy, no doubt, and the quarry is in sight. Only when the season ends, most bags hold shell casings, not meat. And the rifle was borrowed; the bullets, bought on credit.

This trade has more than one honest livelihood. Chasing hot themes is hunting — it earns fast money from volatility: the quarry is running, the shot has to be quick. A hit means a meal; a miss, a hungry day, and back into the woods tomorrow. Rotating between sectors is herding — it earns the market cycle’s money: stop chasing the quarry across the hills, keep livestock in the pen. Markets have their spring, summer, autumn, winter; sell the herd when the grass is lush, buy feed when it withers. Holding a base position and compounding is farming — it earns dividends and compound interest: no hope of an overnight harvest, only faith in the land. Harvests vary in size, but every year there is one. The quarry runs fast and crops grow slow, yet the only thing that has ever fed people is the land. Nobody winters on one meal of game, but a field can feed generations.

One framework puts it this way: beginners hunt, intermediates herd, seasoned investors farm. Easy to mishear. It sounds like a seating chart — hunting at the bottom, farming at the top, hunters somehow lesser. So the objection writes itself: herding and farming are far too slow. By the time the crops ripen, the rally is long gone. A young investor’s capital is thin; without taking a shot, where would a base position even come from? Waiting out the compound curve is for old men.

That question deserves a real answer, not a brush-off like “think long term.” And the answer is: three stages in order of dependence, not of rank. Hunting trains feel for the market — when to fire, when to hold; that instinct can only be paid for in gains and losses. Herding trains feel for the cycle — when to enter, when to exit; that rhythm can only be ground out round by round. Skip the tuition of both and go straight to farming, and the base position will not survive a drawdown — the farmer who never hunted cannot tell his holdings from weeds, and the ones selling at the very bottom are usually those. There is no skipping the tuition; there is only the pricier remedial class. But once the tuition is paid, the trade has to change. Hunting can open the door; it cannot give a place to stand. Whoever makes hunting a lifetime trade books every bit of fast money on credit against the next empty-handed day.

A harder objection follows: does farming have no famine years? Even the house traders live at the mercy of the sky — hasn’t the cycle thesis itself conceded that nothing rises forever? Fair point. Farmers cannot control the weather; when drought or flood arrives, everyone bows. But here is the difference, one character wide: a hunter lives at the mercy of the sky, a farmer lives off it — the gap between a rifle and a field. A hunter waits for quarry to appear; no rain, no quarry, hunter out of the game. His bet is the sky itself. A farmer does not wait for better weather. A farmer waits on the land: famine year, a poor harvest, but the land does not run. While the land is there, winter ends and spring planting resumes on schedule. Markets move in recurring circles — no sector rises forever, none falls forever, like walking a loop around the park pond: peak fades into decline, despair turns the corner toward recovery. The goal is no legendary single strike. The goal is entry and exit done well in every round, rolling the snowball.

So the question was never whether hunting is wrong. It is: after the hunt, then what? Hot themes will return, and the gunfire will not stop. The difference shows only after the season ends: the hunter heads back into the woods, competing with younger riflemen for thinner game; the farmer heads home to his own field, waiting on his own season. The quarry changes every year; the seasons never miss an appointment, and the snowball on the ledger is rolled the same way, lap after lap. The land does not.
