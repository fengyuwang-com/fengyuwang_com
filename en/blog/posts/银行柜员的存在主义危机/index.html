A Bank Teller's Existential Crisis

2025-08-30 ·4 min read ·BusinessSociety

At nine in the morning the shutter rises: the name retires, the staff number clocks in. Six years as a teller is not worth pitying. It is worth dissecting — this job runs every symptom of the service economy through a single window.

Symptom one: time. In year one he watched the clock. By year six he stopped — not from habit, but from switching himself from a person who experiences time to a machine that runs a process: customer arrives, handle, leave. The hard part is never the rush. It is the empty gaps between customers. Behind bulletproof glass, with nothing to do and no one to talk to, only one thing remains: facing his own consciousness. The question “what am I doing” surfaces only in the gaps. Busyness is the anaesthetic; the gaps are the disease.

Symptom two: the product evaporates. Over a hundred transactions a day, hundreds of thousands in cash through his hands, and at quitting time he holds nothing. The money belongs to the bank, the ledger to the system, and even the customer’s thanks is addressed to “the teller”, not to him. His grandfather the carpenter could point at a chair that would stand for decades and say: I made this. The teller’s formula is different — attention, patience, emotion go in; one line on a report comes out, absorbed by the system, gone without a trace. Marx had a word for it. He never needed to read Marx; the desk teaches the vocabulary directly.

Symptom three: the smile is a separate payslip. “Hello, how may I help you” — dozens of times a day, tens of thousands over six years, drifting from genuine to habitual to defensive. Sociologists named this division of labour: emotional work — the hands are not the only thing rented out; facial management is billed separately. When a customer slams the glass and calls the bank a scam, he must smile, apologise, digest. The fault lies in the procedure; the person standing inside the procedure to absorb it is him.

Symptom four: freedom is the sharper pain. In theory he can leave any time: resign, flee to Dali, learn painting, open a small shop. In practice the mortgage is debiting and the child is three. Sartre’s line — man is condemned to be free — reads most cruelly at a teller window. No one holds a gun; therefore every day at the desk is his own choice. Total coercion would be kinder: he could hate the world and stay a clean victim. But he chose: stability selected, risk declined. With no one to blame, the crisis ties itself into a knot — fully aware the life was chosen, unhappy with the result, unable to choose otherwise.

Symptom five: the boulder. Camus insisted we imagine Sisyphus happy; the teller gave the boulder a more precise name: tomorrow, nine AM. Holding his daughter, cooking a meal, an old song at midnight — those moments are real, not absurd. But meaning is short and the boulder is long. The exit is not finding a meaning big enough to forget the stone. It is learning to eat at the same table as meaninglessness without lying to himself.

The objections arrive on schedule. Sentimental drama — is not every office worker the same? — If it were identical everywhere, teller breakdowns would not make separate news: the emotional labour is itemised, the movement limits written into regulation, the error penalties charged to the individual, and automation is a countdown. Branches close by the year, headcount shrinks, the gaps between customers shorten, the crisis densifies. The inverse trend holds too: on the day the window stops needing people, the existential crisis graduates from literature into unemployment statistics.

No prescription here; quit-and-follow-your-dream is the cheapest placebo there is. The window’s crisis is the shared staff badge of all labour whose product cannot stay in a thing: the weekly report, the assembly-line cadence, the chat queue behind a screen. Different numbers, same formula. Reading someone else’s numbered crisis with a cold spine means recognising that your own badge carries the same print, filed elsewhere.
