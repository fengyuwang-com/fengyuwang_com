Recognized, Not Watched

2026-09-11 · Marketing

Same mall, same recognition system, two kinds of message. A member walks into Langham Place and the phone buzzes at once: welcome back, Gene — followed by a list of offers only members can claim. The list arrives before any store has been entered. Passing an American Eagle store brings another: a members-only special, the store on the left, twenty percent off today. The welcome and the store advertisement come from the same system. Recognizing a person is service; shadowing a person is business. The dividing line is not the technology — so where is it?

The member signed; the passerby did not

Someone will say: drop the pretense — member offers and targeted ads are the same thing, a face traded for a transaction. The mechanism is indeed shared; the contract is not. The member registered — registration completed on the phone, by the person — knows recognition happens, trades for offers with a clear price tag, and can opt out at any time. The passerby signed nothing. The system still runs age and gender detection on people walking by, and the ads switch accordingly, with zero awareness throughout. The difference is not the data flow. It is whether any tacit consent exists.

Among the passersby, not one heard a prompt or got a receipt, not even a greeting. The face was read once, and its owner is the last to know.

Banning technology fails to govern use

So ban it all? What gets banned is the welcome back and the ability to find a missing person, while the camera that cannot tell who its master is survives. Banning the technology instead of judging its uses punishes those who use it lawfully and fails to stop those who use it in secret. What needs governing is a list of uses: which scenes require prior registration, and which may never deploy it at all.

Consent can be built into the product

Another objection: does registration really count as informed? A stack of terms nobody reads — ticking the box is selling out. That objection is half right, and it also points at the road. Consent is not a two-hundred-page agreement handed over; it is the moment the machine says the name — a person knowing they are being recognized right now. The companies that can afford the business of recognizing customers are precisely the ones best placed to build consent into the product: the first push upon entering the mall states its source and carries an exit. The ones that cannot are treating consent as a cost.

What shadows people is not the message; it is the absence of an off switch

Label the source, and then what? The pushes keep coming, and the privacy is still given away. Labeling changes the position of power: knowing where a message comes from, and whether it can be turned off, turns the push from ambiance into an offer. The true feeling of being watched comes from having nowhere to close it, not from receiving the message itself. Transparency does not shrink the data; it moves the decision back to the person.

The first line of the rule

Technology only does the recognizing. Whether recognition turns into welcome or into shadowing is decided by rules — and the rules can be short. The first line of those rules should read: who knows, and who benefits. With that line written in, the camera recognizes guests. Without it, every face it finds is a target.
